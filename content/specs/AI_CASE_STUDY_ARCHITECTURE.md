## Architecture decision

The illustration, copywriting, and prototyping work will become three independent case studies.

- They will not sit under a public Branch AI parent page.
- Each case study will have its own title, premise, route, homepage card, hero, metadata, five-step progress rail, and outcome.
- Public titles should describe the problem, scale, or transformation. Repeating `Branch AI` across all three titles is not allowed.
- The three cases may link to one another as related work, but they should not be presented as chapters that require a specific reading order.
- The current combined case study is preserved as source material until the standalone replacements are approved. It should then become an unlinked archive.

## Illustration case study: five-step structure

### 1. The problem

Progress label: `The problem`

- Why Branch needed illustrations.
- What inconsistency looked like in the product.
- Who experienced the problem.
- Why the problem mattered to product quality, speed, and brand trust.
- Evidence that established the problem.

### 2. Defining consistency

Progress label: `Defining consistency`

- What consistent needed to mean without making every image identical.
- The visual qualities the team evaluated.
- Success criteria and non-negotiables.
- Early research, references, and competing visual directions.

### 3. Choosing a direction

Progress label: `Choosing a direction`

- Approaches considered.
- The strongest rejected or abandoned direction.
- Trade-offs and constraints.
- How review sessions and the FigJam vote informed the decision.
- Why the selected direction gave the team the best foundation.

### 4. Building the system

Progress label: `Building the system`

- Instructions, manifest, JSON rules, material catalogue, and render pipeline.
- Render Mode and Ideation Mode.
- Colour, material, camera, lighting, composition, and realism constraints.
- Six major system versions and more than 500 generation iterations.
- How repeated failures became rules.
- How human review stayed inside the system.

### 5. What changed

Progress label: `What changed`

- The final workflow and representative outputs.
- What became faster, clearer, or more consistent.
- Rollout across product markets.
- Ownership and collaboration.
- Evidence limitations.
- The most useful learning and the next opportunity.

## Narrative principle

The page should show design thinking without mechanically listing a generic design process. Every section must answer at least one of these questions:

1. What problem or risk became clearer?
2. What decision did the evidence change?
3. What constraint shaped the system?
4. What did Olumide personally build or decide?
5. What became different because of the work?

If a paragraph does not answer one of those questions, it probably does not belong in the case study.

## Writing standard

The illustration case study must feel passionate, informed, simple, and concise.

- Use first person for confirmed personal work.
- Build emotion from the stakes, failures, decisions, and progress.
- Show depth through reasoning and evidence.
- Prefer short sentences and short paragraphs.
- Remove repeated facts and repeated conclusions.
- Keep headings descriptive enough to carry the story on their own.
- Do not use em dashes.
- Do not invent a metric, timeframe, role, or outcome.

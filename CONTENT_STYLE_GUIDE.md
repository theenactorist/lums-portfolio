# Theme 2 Content Style Guide

Status: Active editorial standard  
Last updated: 2026-06-26  
Applies to: Theme 2 public portfolio copy

## Purpose

This guide defines how Theme 2 should sound before copy enters React components. It combines the seniority of Charlie Hart's presentation, the expressiveness of Lucas Barrientos's case-study storytelling, and Olumide's own evidence-backed voice.

It is a voice reference, not permission to copy language from either portfolio.

## The voice in one sentence

Calm seniority with enough narrative energy to make the decisions, tension, and human work memorable.

## Voice model

| Quality | What it means here | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Senior | Selective detail, clear judgment, direct outcomes, visible trade-offs | Explaining every design activity or trying to prove seniority with titles |
| Expressive | Concrete tension, turning points, contrast, and reflection | Hype, melodrama, metaphors that obscure the work |
| Specific | Named products, users, constraints, decisions, and approved metrics | Generic claims such as `improved the experience` |
| Personal | Clear first-person ownership where confirmed | Taking credit for management or team work |
| Business-aware | Connect user problems to product, operational, or commercial consequences | Treating the interface as the only outcome |
| Human | Warm, readable, candid, and occasionally conversational | Corporate jargon or robotic case-study prose |

## Seniority rules

- Start with the point, not the chronology.
- Select the decisions that changed the trajectory; omit routine production detail unless it proves scope.
- State what Olumide chose, why he chose it, and what changed.
- Show boundaries: what he owned, what the team owned, and what remained unresolved.
- Include limitations without apologising for them.
- Prefer calm statements over self-congratulation.

### Preferred

> The team was already using AI. The problem was that every output carried a different version of Branch.

### Avoid

> I was tasked with leveraging cutting-edge AI tools to revolutionise the design process.

## Expressiveness rules

- Establish the tension early.
- Use contrast: before/after, expectation/reality, speed/quality, individual/team, prompt/system.
- Name the moment when the brief changed.
- Let short sentences carry major turning points.
- Follow expressive lines with evidence.
- Use concrete product language instead of abstract inspiration.

### Preferred

> Better prompts helped once. Branch needed a system that would still work for the next designer, market, and release.

### Avoid

> This marked the beginning of an exciting and transformative journey.

## Olumide's perspective

Recurring themes that are appropriate when supported by the project:

- Behavioural science and how people interpret choices, risk, progress, and feedback.
- Systems thinking across interfaces, design systems, teams, and implementation.
- AI as reusable product infrastructure rather than novelty.
- Comfort moving between research, product strategy, interface craft, prototyping, and design QA.
- Designing across fintech, payments, lending, crypto, enterprise systems, and multiple markets.

Do not force these themes into a project when the evidence does not support them.

## Ownership language

### Use `I` for confirmed personal work

- `I ran the product-team poll.`
- `I translated recurring feedback into system rules.`
- `I designed and tested the workflow.`

### Use `we` for confirmed shared work

- `We selected the final direction after three review sessions.`
- `We shipped the first set across the India product.`

### Name collaborators when their contribution matters

- `Deovrat sponsored the programme and reviewed the direction.`
- `I worked with product managers and engineers to validate the workflow.`

These are structural examples only. Do not use them as project facts until the relevant fact sheet is approved.

### Never use ambiguous ownership

Avoid phrases such as:

- `I drove the team to...`
- `I transformed the organisation...`
- `My work increased...`

unless the scope and attribution are verified.

## Outcome and metric language

- Include the baseline, result, timeframe, and source when available.
- Distinguish measured results from directional feedback.
- Use `contributed to` when multiple factors affected the outcome.
- Use `the team reported` or `directional feedback suggested` when no formal measurement exists.
- Do not convert estimates into exact results.
- Do not combine separate metrics because they share the same number.

### Preferred

> Directional feedback from product managers suggested that interactive prototypes reduced PRD evaluation time by about 30%.

### Avoid

> The prototyper made the team 30% more efficient.

## Page-level tone

| Surface | Tone | Density |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Navigation and CTAs | Literal and economical | Minimal |
| Homepage hero | Calm, memorable, immediately legible | Very low |
| Homepage project cards | Outcome-led and specific | Low |
| About | Personal, reflective, confident | Medium |
| Case-study hero | Consequence and transformation first | Low |
| Case-study body | Expressive but evidence-led | Medium to high |
| Résumé | Factual and compact | High |
| Ask Lums | Warm, concise, useful | Low to medium |
| 404 | Brief and lightly playful | Very low |

## Case-study narrative pattern

Use this as a writing rhythm, not a mandatory list of headings:

1. Outcome or changed state
2. Situation and stakes
3. What kept failing
4. Evidence and insight
5. Strategic decision
6. Consequential design decisions
7. Testing and iteration
8. Result and attribution
9. What changed in the thinking

Project-specific phase labels must be approved before prose is implemented.

## Sentence and paragraph guidance

- Prefer active voice.
- Use one main idea per paragraph.
- Mix short turning-point sentences with longer explanatory sentences.
- Keep headings literal and informative.
- Avoid starting consecutive paragraphs with `I`.
- Keep text links descriptive.
- Spell out unfamiliar acronyms on first use.
- Use en dashes for ranges and em dashes sparingly for emphasis.

## Canadian English

Use Canadian spelling and conventions consistently:

- `behaviour`, `colour`, `centre`
- `organise` or `organization` only according to the established copy choice; do not mix forms within a page
- Dates in clear month-name format where ambiguity is possible
- Sentence case for headings and buttons

Product names and quoted source text retain their official spelling.

## Words and patterns to avoid

- `pixel-perfect`
- `seamless` without evidence
- `revolutionary`
- `game-changing`
- `leveraged`
- `utilised` when `used` is clearer
- `users loved it` without evidence
- `single-handedly`
- `end-to-end` repeated as filler
- `I was tasked with`
- `the design process` as a substitute for an actual decision
- generic Double Diamond narration

## Quotes and recommendations

- Preserve approved wording and attribution.
- Do not rewrite a recommendation in the speaker's voice.
- Correct only obvious transcription errors and only with approval.
- Separate recommendation language from project-fact evidence.

## Editorial review checklist

Before copy is marked approved, confirm:

- The opening communicates the point without supporting context.
- Every personal claim has confirmed ownership.
- Every number matches an approved fact-sheet entry.
- Team outcomes use team language.
- The copy identifies at least one meaningful decision or trade-off.
- Expressive language is followed by evidence.
- Headings describe the section's actual point.
- CTAs use the approved inventory.
- Canadian English is consistent.
- Paragraphs remain readable at mobile widths.
- The copy does not imitate a reference portfolio's wording.

## Approval statuses

- `Draft`: editorial work in progress.
- `Needs fact approval`: blocked by factual or ownership uncertainty.
- `Ready for copy review`: facts are approved; wording needs review.
- `Approved`: safe to implement without changing meaning.
- `Implemented`: matches the approved copy in the rendered portfolio.
